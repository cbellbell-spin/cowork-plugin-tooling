# Working With Me
**Chris Bell | March 2026 — Working Draft**

---

This is not a formal document. It is a practical one. It captures how I think, what I expect from the people I work with, and what you can expect from me. Read it when you join. Come back to it when something feels off. Push back on it if you think I'm wrong — including on this document itself.

---

## What I Believe

A few things I hold to be true about the PM craft:

**PMs are supposed to have opinions.** On the product, on the market, on the customer, on the tradeoff in front of the team right now. Not reflexive opinions - informed ones. Presenting options is not enough. Anyone can list the options. The PM's job is to have a recommendation and be able to defend it: here is my answer, here is why, here are the alternatives I considered, here is the data I used to decide. Lead with the conclusion. The people you are presenting to are busy - do not make them decode your thinking.

**Alignment is not consensus.** Driving alignment means getting people pointed in the same direction, often without everyone agreeing. Consensus feels safer. It is often slower and frequently produces worse outcomes. Know the difference.

**Responsibility usually exceeds authority.** PMs rarely control everything that needs to happen for a product to succeed. That means influence is the job - understanding what other people care about, meeting them there, and building the coalitions necessary to make progress.

**Always dig below the surface with customers.** A customer request taken at face value is a specification for the wrong product. Your job is to understand the pressure behind the ask, the workflow it lives in, and the problem they cannot quite articulate. This is true for every customer interaction, not just the ones that seem off.

**Stability is a feature.** Reliability, performance, and debt reduction are not distractions from "real" product work. They are product work. Part of your job is helping the organization understand that and prioritize accordingly.

**PMs should be perpetually dissatisfied.** There should always be a gap between how things are and how they could be, and you should feel that gap. That restlessness is the engine. But it has to coexist with the judgment to know when something is good enough - when the team needs to ship and move on. Knowing when to say "good enough, for now" is a skill, not a surrender.

**Balance short and long term.** Know what needs to get solved fast and what can take time. Sometimes cutting corners is the right call - but when you do, three things are required: discuss it with the team, document it explicitly, and create space in a future sprint to unwind it. A hack that nobody knows about is not a tradeoff, it is a debt you are hiding.

## How I Expect You to Work

**Answer first.** When you are communicating a decision, a recommendation, or a request for help, lead with the conclusion. Give me the answer, then the reasoning, then the supporting detail - in that order. Not the other way around.

```
        ┌─────────────────┐
        │   The Answer    │   ← Start here. Always.
        └────────┬────────┘
                 │
        ┌────────┴────────┐
        │   Key Reasons   │   ← Why is this the right answer?
        └────────┬────────┘
                 │
        ┌────────┴────────┐
        │  Data / Detail  │   ← Evidence, alternatives, caveats
        └─────────────────┘
```

An exec who lives in P&L language needs a different version of the story than an engineering lead who thinks in systems. The logic is the same; the framing is not. It is your job to make that translation. If you want to go deeper on this structure, look up the Minto Pyramid Principle.

**Start rough, then refine.** When developing a new idea - a business case, a design concept, a feature - start at the lowest fidelity that still communicates it. Think of a four-year-old drawing a house with a crayon: you can tell what it is, but the fidelity is low. That is the point. Low fidelity invites input; nobody falls in love with a crayon drawing, which means you can change it without friction. Refine only as much as the next round of feedback requires. Over-investing early is one of the most common and costly mistakes in product work.

**Bring a point of view, not just a problem.** When you come to me with an issue, come with your read on it. What do you think is happening, what have you considered, what would you do? I will push on your thinking - that is how we both get smarter. Showing up without a view is showing up unprepared.

**Know how to say no.** Part of the job is protecting the team's capacity and the product's integrity - and that includes pushing back on me. "No" is a legitimate answer. So is "not right now" or "yes, but here are the tradeoffs - which of your current priorities should we revisit?" This applies whether the pressure is coming from a customer, from sales, or from me. If I am asking for something that does not make sense or that crowds out more important work, say so. I would rather hear it than have you absorb a bad ask silently. Your time is also a resource worth defending. A PM who attends every meeting, fields every request, and says yes to everything is not being helpful - they are being ineffective.

**Disagree, then commit.** Once we have worked through an issue and a decision has been made, carry it forward as if it were your own. You can have your objection on the record - I want it there, it may be right - but relitigating settled decisions in adjacent conversations is not principled. It undermines the team's ability to execute.

**Match decision speed to decision cost.** The lower the cost of being wrong, the faster and more autonomously you should decide. Two-way-door decisions - the ones you can reverse - should not wait for me. Make the call, use your peers or working team for a quick sanity check if you want one, and move. Reserve the heavier process for the decisions that are genuinely hard to undo.

**The non-negotiables.** Most things are coachable. A few are not. I have a hard no-asshole rule: how you treat people - engineers, sales, customers, each other - is not separable from how good you are at your job. I also expect you to deliver what you say you will deliver; surprises happen, avoidable misses without warning do not. These are not performance issues. They are the terms of being on this team.

## A Writing Culture

We start with writing. Before a meeting, before a decision, before a major ask - there should be a short document that describes the situation (including the goals), the proposed path forward, and the options considered. The act of writing forces clarity. It also respects other people's time: a good doc lets someone engage on their own schedule instead of needing a synchronous meeting to get up to speed.

If a meeting is required after a doc has circulated, amend the same document with the decision, the next steps, and the timelines. The doc is the record.

There is no platonic ideal of a perfect document. Amazon's one-pagers and six-pagers are a reasonable model to draw from, not a standard to stress about. The goal is a document that is clear enough to invite real discussion - not one that signals effort. Take an answer-first approach. Keep it as short as it can be while still being complete. Share it as an invitation for comments, not a finished position.

AI is genuinely useful here. Use it to turn a brain dump into a structured draft, pressure-test your logic, or find the gaps in your argument before anyone else does. A first draft that would have taken two hours can take twenty minutes. Use that.

## Day-to-Day Communication

We are all drowning in inbound. Before you choose a channel, think about what you actually need:

- How urgently do you need a response?
- How important is this - is it a one-way door?
- How likely am I to have what I need to respond off the top of my head?
- How much discussion does this actually warrant?

**If you need my immediate attention:** call, text, or DM me. That is what those channels are for.

**If you need a real discussion:** book time or swing by. Getting my attention is not the same as creating room for a conversation. If the topic warrants actual back-and-forth, pick a high-bandwidth channel. A DM thread is not a substitute for a real conversation.

**If it is low urgency:** email or a shared doc. Let me engage when I have the context and time to engage properly.

Match the channel to the need. Over-escalating trains me to ignore escalations. Under-escalating buries things that matter.

## How I Work With You

**My job is to make you successful.** Your success is my success - not the other way around. I want you to be seen as the domain expert in your area. In business reviews and stakeholder meetings, you lead. I am there to orchestrate, to handle the moments that need my seniority, and to have your back if things go sideways.

**If you see me jump in during your presentation**, know that I am trying to keep us off the rocks - I likely have context you do not, or I can see something about how the room is reading the discussion. I will usually start with a leading question to get you back on track, or reframe something in a way I think will land better. My goal is to get you back in the driver's seat, not to take over. If I need to take over, I will say so - directly and without drama.

**Reviews should be about the hard stuff.** Organize meetings around the topics that actually need input - the tricky tradeoffs, the places you are stuck, the decisions that need broader context or authority. We can read routine updates in a doc. If a meeting could have been an email, it should have been.

**1:1s are yours.** I use 1:1s to work through what is on your mind, not to get status updates. My default is Socratic - I am more interested in helping you reason through a problem than in telling you the answer. Come with what you are actually wrestling with.

**I will tell you where you stand.** I believe in direct feedback, and I will give it in different settings depending on what makes sense. Sometimes I will share it in a group - not to embarrass you, but because the learning is useful for the whole team and the moment calls for it. Other times I will save it for a 1:1 where we can get into it properly. Either way, the intent is the same: to help you get better. If you want to unpack feedback further, set up time and we will have that conversation.

**I own my mistakes.** When I am wrong - and I will be - I will say so specifically: here is what I thought, here is what the data or the outcome told me, here is what I would do differently. I expect the same from you.

**If you think I am not living up to any of this, tell me.** This document is a commitment, not a marketing piece. If you see a gap between what I have written here and how I am actually operating, I want to know. That conversation will not go badly for you.

## What Good Looks Like

The PMs who thrive in my teams tend to share a few things. They develop genuine expertise in their area and come to me for broader context or perspective, not for answers to questions they should own. They understand that making their manager more effective is part of their job - not out of politics, but because that is how organizations work. They make others confident - me, their partners, their peers, their customers - that things in their area are under control, moving forward, and being thought about well. And they bring an energy that makes the people around them better - they are curious, they ask the questions that move the discussion forward, they are excited to turn their dissatisfactions into actual improvements rather than just complaints, and they help the team find better ideas than anyone would have reached alone. They live the culture rather than just agreeing with it.

If you are not thriving here, that is worth a real conversation - not a performance review. I think about what I want for the people on my team in this order: first, that they are happy, successful, and thriving as people. Second, that they are happy, successful, and thriving in this business and in their careers. Third, that they are thriving on my team specifically. If the first two are not being met, the third does not matter much. My job is to help you succeed - and if the environment we have created together is getting in the way of that, something needs to change.

It may be that this is not the right role or the right environment for you to do your best work, and that has nothing to do with your ability or your potential. Think of it like a plant in the wrong pot: the plant is not broken, it just needs different conditions. Whatever happens, I will do what I can to help you get where you are trying to go.
